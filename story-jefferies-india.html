Jefferies moves more money to India, less to chip-heavy Taiwan, Korea

Jefferies' strategist Christopher Wood thinks big-tech spending on AI data centres may be close to its top. Markets full of chip companies could feel that first; India has far fewer of them.

Semicon Weekly · 10 November 2026
ShareWhatsAppTelegram
In short
  • Jefferies has raised India's share in its Asia (ex-Japan) portfolio to 14%.
  • It suggests owning less of Taiwan and South Korea, whose stock markets depend a lot on chip makers.
  • Its strategist Christopher Wood worries that AI spending by big tech may be close to its peak.

Key numbers

What happened

  • Global broker Jefferies raised India's weight in its Asia-Pacific (ex-Japan) portfolio to 14%, reports said on 7–9 November 2026.
  • It suggested cutting exposure to Taiwan and South Korea, where big chip makers make up a large part of the stock market.
  • Jefferies' strategist Christopher Wood said AI spending by big tech firms may be close to its peak. Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta together spent about $202 billion on capital expenditure in July–September, 108% more than a year earlier.
  • Memory chip shares have already fallen about 40% from their June high, according to the IANS report.

Why it matters

Why this is useful

  • When investors worry about AI spending, chip company shares fall first, and hiring plans can slow for a while.
  • India's market has few listed chip makers, so investors see it as a way to stay in Asia with less AI risk.

Why it is good for freshers

  • AI chip spending may slow for a while, so freshers should build strong basics that work across design, test and manufacturing, not only AI chips.
  • Chip hiring goes in cycles: keep applying widely — auto, power, IoT and memory test roles stay steady when AI cools.
  • Use this time to build projects and certifications so you are ready when hiring picks up again.

Why it is good for India

  • Money moving into Indian markets can help Indian companies raise funds, including electronics and chip-related ones.
  • India's chip plan is long-term and mostly government-backed, so short market swings matter less for its fabs and design schemes.
