1.What are the main steps of the physical design flow?

Show answer
  • Import the netlist, libraries and timing constraints
  • Floorplan (die size, IOs, macros)
  • Power planning (rings, straps, rails)
  • Placement of standard cells
  • Clock tree synthesis
  • Routing (global then detailed)
  • Optimization and timing closure
  • Signoff checks: STA, DRC, LVS, antenna, IR drop and electromigration
  • Then GDSII for tape-out

Common mistake: Putting CTS before placement; forgetting power planning or signoff checks

2.What is floorplanning, and what decisions do you make in it?

Show answer
  • Choose die and core size, aspect ratio and target utilization
  • Place IO pins/pads
  • Place macros (memories, IP) usually near the edges with channels between them and pins facing the core logic
  • Add placement and routing blockages
  • Plan the power grid
  • A poor floorplan causes congestion and timing problems that later steps cannot fix

Common mistake: Placing macros in the middle with no channels; ignoring macro pin direction

3.What is clock tree synthesis, and what does it try to optimize?

Show answer
  • CTS builds a tree of buffers/inverters that delivers the clock from its source to every flop
  • goals: low skew between sinks, controlled insertion delay (latency), good transition times, and low power and area
  • Before CTS the clock is ideal
  • After CTS real skew is known, so timing is updated and hold is fixed
  • Special rules like wider spacing (NDR) or shielding protect clock nets

Common mistake: Saying CTS only minimizes latency; forgetting that hold must be checked again after CTS

4.What is utilization, and why is the starting utilization not close to 100%?

Show answer
  • utilization = area of standard cells (and macros) / core area
  • Designs usually start around 60–75%
  • Free space is needed for buffers added during optimization, the clock tree, routing tracks, hold fixing and later ECOs
  • Too high utilization causes congestion and timing failures

Common mistake: Thinking 100% utilization is best because the chip is smaller

5.What are DRC and LVS checks?

Show answer
  • DRC (design rule check) checks the layout against the foundry's manufacturing rules such as minimum width, spacing, enclosure and density
  • LVS (layout versus schematic) extracts devices and connections from the layout and compares them with the netlist to make sure they match (no opens, shorts or missing devices)
  • Both must be clean before tape-out

Common mistake: Swapping the meanings of DRC and LVS; thinking a DRC-clean layout is always correct

6.What is routing congestion, and how can you reduce it?

Show answer
  • Congestion happens when an area needs more routing tracks than are available
  • It causes detours, longer wires, DRC violations and timing problems
  • fixes: better macro placement and wider channels, placement blockages or cell padding to spread dense logic, lower local utilization, reduce high pin-density cells, change the floorplan or pin placement

Common mistake: Only increasing utilization; ignoring congestion until detailed routing

7.What are IR drop and electromigration (EM)?

Show answer
  • IR drop is the voltage loss in the power grid (current × resistance), so cells get less than full VDD and become slower
  • It can be static (average current) or dynamic (switching bursts)
  • EM is the slow movement of metal atoms caused by high current density over time, which can create opens or shorts
  • fixes: wider or more power straps, more vias, decoupling capacitors, spreading high-activity cells

Common mistake: Saying IR drop only affects power, not timing; confusing EM with crosstalk

8.What is crosstalk, and how does it affect timing?

Show answer
  • Crosstalk comes from coupling capacitance between neighbouring wires
  • When an aggressor switches it can speed up or slow down a victim net that is switching (delay change), or put a glitch on a quiet victim (noise)
  • Slow-down can cause setup failures and speed-up can cause hold failures
  • fixes: more spacing, shielding with ground wires, upsizing the victim driver, changing layers, reducing parallel wire length

Common mistake: Thinking crosstalk only causes glitches, not delay changes

9.What is the antenna effect, and how is it fixed?

Show answer
  • During manufacturing, a long metal wire connected to a transistor gate can collect charge from plasma etching before the driver is connected
  • This charge can damage the thin gate oxide
  • Antenna rules limit the ratio of metal area to gate area
  • fixes: break the wire and jump to a higher metal layer near the gate, add an antenna (reverse-biased) diode at the gate, or insert a buffer close to the gate

Common mistake: Saying antenna violations matter for the finished chip's normal operation; adding the diode at the driver instead of the gate

10.What are standard cells, well tap cells, end-cap cells and filler cells?

Show answer
  • Standard cells are library gates and flops with a fixed height that sit in rows
  • Well tap cells connect the N-well to VDD and the substrate to VSS at regular spacing to prevent latch-up
  • End-cap cells sit at the ends of rows to protect the cell edges and well boundaries
  • Filler cells fill empty gaps in rows to keep wells and implants continuous
  • Decap cells add local charge to reduce dynamic IR drop

Common mistake: Confusing tap cells with fillers; thinking fillers add logic
